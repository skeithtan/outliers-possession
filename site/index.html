At a glance

Possession from the tracking alone

The engine reads the automatic tracking of the match and says, for every frame, which team has the ball or that the ball is dead. Two models, trained on labelled external matches from three providers, give the probability of each label: a small recurrent neural network and a per-frame model of gradient-boosted trees. A Viterbi decoder that penalises every change decodes their weighted average. Neither the labels nor the video of this match shaped the models or any setting. Both models run in numpy. Every gain below is measured against our rule engine, an earlier engine of physical rules that one configuration setting restores.

Compliance statement

  • No frame of this match is labelled by hand, and no other data about it is made by people.
  • No label and no video of this match trained, tuned or selected anything. The public window served only pipeline checks: the validator, the frame alignment and byte-for-byte reproduction. We scored the engine there once, after its settings were final (Section 03).
  • The two models trained only on Metrica sample games, Sportec Open DFL (DFL) and SkillCorner, and every setting of the models, the blend and the decoder was chosen on external matches. No PFF match trained either model of the engine: 43 helped us compare designs, and 15 were held out (Section 03).
  • On this match we checked the output without labels, against limits taken from external matches and written down before the run. Two other networks failed these checks and are not used (Section 03).
  • The parts kept from the rule engine, the ball cleaning, the goalkeeper-swap correction and the dead ball before each kickoff, use no setting fitted to labels. The match data is never committed to the repository.

We keep a record of the source and the history of every setting.

01 The approach, and why

Count possessions, not frames

spellF1 matches whole possessions, not frames. One short false possession costs as much as a long missed one, and every stoppage ends a possession. Possession is an event, not a distance, as in the definitions of Metrica, Second Spectrum, PFF and Link and Hoernig (2017).

The public window cannot choose

On the public window, smooth125 beats smooth50. On the full match the order reverses. Seventeen possessions are too few to choose between two settings.

So the public window only checks the pipeline. No setting of the engine comes from it.

A spell is a run of H or of A

The ground truth is on top and our labels are below. A band joins two spells of the same team that overlap. Its strength is their temporal IoU: the overlap divided by the union.

One to one, at five thresholds

The scorer matches spells greedily, by descending IoU, and each spell matches once. It does this at IoU 0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6 and 0.7, then averages the five F1 scores.

Scroll, or drag the handle, to move the threshold.

Short spells are fragile

The shortest public spell lasts 19 frames. Shift a copy by a few frames and its IoU with the original falls fast. Drag the copy, or scroll.

Timing is part of the score

The nearest player changes before the true owner does. The baseline follows it. The public window holds 20 transitions: 12 hand-overs, 4 stoppage starts and 4 restarts. The engine has a transition within 25 frames (1 s) of 11 of them, and the hand-overs it matches land a median of 3.5 frames early. The chart counts the same for the baseline.

02 Missing and noisy tracking

The ball is visible on 59.3% of graded frames

The tracker loses the ball for minutes, smooths it at every contact and sometimes swaps the goalkeepers' tracks. The engine cleans what it can before it computes any feature. The models learn the rest from external matches with the same kinds of faults added.

Ball gaps of minutes

The 133 gaps longer than 2 s cover 40.6% of graded frames, with a median of 11 s, so interpolation recovers nothing. The models see whether the ball is visible, how long ago it was last seen and how long until it is next seen.

The network alone missed stoppages while the ball was missing: on the PFF design matches it labelled only 82.5% of such dead frames D. Averaged in, the per-frame model raised spellF1 on 43 of the 43 PFF design matches. We write X only on frames that are never graded.

Defects added in training

Half of the training windows come from one of 16 copies of each match with tracking defects added: a hidden ball, dropped players, tracks given to the wrong team, duplicated tracks and fragmented tracks. The ranges of the defects are generic. None is measured on this match.

On live frames where we hide the ball, the engine calls D on 20% to 21% of them on the three providers, against 45% to 56% for the rule engine.

A ball that the tracker holds still

DFL's tracking holds the ball exactly still while play is dead, which no other tracker does. A ball that does not move for 13 frames or more is removed. In training, half of those runs stay visible instead, with a small jitter measured on SkillCorner's ball during stoppages, so that the network also sees stoppages with the ball visible.

Without that jitter, a network that had never seen SkillCorner labelled only 30% of such a stoppage as dead (rule engine: 67%). With it, 60%.

A smoothed ball and swapped goalkeepers

The tracker smooths the ball over about 0.9 s. A contact shows as a bump of acceleration about 24 frames wide, and the smoothing moves the ball off the foot at every change of velocity, by about 2.3 m for a 15 m/s pass from rest.

Before any feature, the engine counts the 181 frames where the ball jumps more than 3 m in one frame as missing. The tracker sometimes carries one goalkeeper's track onto the other goalkeeper, and the engine moves the position back, on 7,999 graded frames: the goalkeeper-swap correction. This ball cleaning, the goalkeeper-swap correction and the players' speed from their positions are the parts of the rule engine's code that the engine keeps.

03 Results

Results on matches the models never saw

The rule engine loses most on home-or-away decisions, and a model can learn them from labelled matches. No label of this match may train it, and the public window is too short to choose anything. So we test every choice on external matches, which come with their providers' labels: the eight Metrica and DFL training matches (three Metrica sample games, five DFL matches), 20 SkillCorner matches and 58 PFF FC World Cup 2022 matches. A gain is the engine's spellF1 minus the rule engine's, averaged over a test's matches.

SkillCorner labels its matches two ways: by its phases of play, and by its possession field, which names a team on each frame of its tracking. With an unseen provider, no model that labels a match saw any match of its provider. No PFF match trained a model. The 43 PFF design matches helped us compare designs, so they are not an untouched test. The 15 held-out PFF matches were kept out of every design choice and run once. On PFF we score the 2-second labels, in which a team's run under 2 s inside live play does not change the possession, and the labels as provided.

03 Results / on this match

On this match, checks that need no labels

The output has 264 possessions (rule engine: 279), D on 46.6% of graded frames (48.6%) in 103 stoppages (97), and a median possession of 7.5 s (8.3 s). We checked it against limits that come from external matches and were written down before the run. Such a check had stopped two other networks: one whose seeds disagreed too much, one whose possessions ran longer than in any external labels.

The two models met every limit. The four networks give a different most likely class on 5.18% of graded frames (limit 5.48%), and the four mirrored copies on 2.01% (limit 2.11%). Possession lengths stay inside the bounds of the external labels, and the 265 possessions that the models wrote are 0.950 times the rule engine's 279, inside the range of 0.9464 to 1.387 on the external matches. A check of the output against Law 8 then showed that the models gave the first 0.84 s of the second half to the team that had kicked off the first half. The kickoff team rule removes that possession, so the output has 264, 0.9462 times the rule engine's and 0.0002 below that range. Effective playing time in LaLiga, about 53 of 106 minutes, makes a D share of 35% to 50% plausible. The chart's allowed ranges are wider, because they catch only a broken output.

05 Limitations, and what next

What the engine still gets wrong

The two models have no held-out result of their own. On this match they end the restarts after goals early, and in the public window they miss two short possessions. We do not know where the organisers' labels sit between frame-level labels and coarser ones, so the decoder's costs are a compromise.

No held-out result for the two models

The 15 held-out PFF matches ran once, on the earlier pair of models, with the same design and settings but an earlier attacking-direction rule: +0.170 over the rule engine, above it on 15 of 15. The two models of the engine were not run there (Section 03).

SkillCorner's possession field

With SkillCorner unseen, the engine is only level with the rule engine on SkillCorner's possession field (+0.004, above it on 12 of 20 matches), and it splits more possessions: 41% of its possessions share a labelled possession with another, against 36%. In stoppages with the ball visible it labels about 57% of the stoppage as dead, against 67% for the rule engine. If this match's labels cut possessions as finely, the engine may split some here too, and it may label too little of a stoppage as dead while the ball stays visible.

Restarts after goals end early

The frames before the kickoff of each half are dead ball, and the first 1 s after it goes to the team that takes the kickoff. The models find every other restart. The engine does not read Smart Tagging. We compared its output with the four goals that Smart Tagging marks: after each goal the engine gives the kickoff to the team that the tag does not name for the goal, as it should if the tag names the scorer, which we assume. It ends those stoppages early: by 2 to 3 frames at three of them and by 2.8 s at one. The rule engine places those kickoffs on a calibrated frame.

Short possessions in the public window

The engine finds 6 of the 12 public hand-overs, changes of possession straight from one team to the other, within 25 frames. Four of its six misses are two short possessions of the home team, 19 and 33 frames long (0.76 s and 1.32 s), that it does not split from the away team's possession around them (frames 15346 to 15365 and 15766 to 15799). The decoder charges 12 for each change of team, so a short possession needs strong evidence.

Four of the twelve hand-overs (frames 11351, 11553, 13785 and 15766) have no player of the new team within 1.5 m of the tracked ball near the change. The engine finds two of them, 11553 and 13785.

The decoder's costs

We do not know where the organisers' labels sit between frame-level labels and coarser ones. Costs that suit the frame-level labels of Metrica and DFL differ from those that suit PFF's coarser labels, and 12 for each kind of change is a compromise between them.

The possession count

Its ratio to the rule engine's count is 0.9462, 0.0002 below the range of the external matches (0.9464 to 1.387), after the kickoff team rule (Section 03).

Next, with more time

The restart after a goal, the home-or-away decisions on SkillCorner's possession field, and a run of this match on Intel or AMD hardware.

The engine needs Python 3.9 to 3.12, two pinned packages and the organisers' data bundle. It reads the tactical ATD and games.py, and loads two weights files from engine/models/. It never trains. Two runs and a fresh clone give the same md5. One configuration setting restores the rule engine and writes its labels instead.
